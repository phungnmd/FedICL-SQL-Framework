\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

We address four research questions. \textbf{RQ1}: does \method{}
improve on FedAvg SFT and the other baselines at the same number of
rounds? \textbf{RQ2}: does the gain carry over to other models and
federated setups? \textbf{RQ3}: does it hold on perturbed questions and
under test-suite accuracy? \textbf{RQ4}: what changes in the generated
queries, and what does a round cost a client? Section~\ref{sec:results}
answers them in this order.

\subsection{Datasets and metrics}\label{sec:datasets}

We use two cross-domain benchmarks, BIRD~\cite{li2023bird} and
Spider~\cite{yu2018spider}. The clients hold the training set of a
benchmark and the model is evaluated on its evaluation set, whose
databases no client has seen, as the two sets share none. We evaluate on
BIRD dev (\fact{questions.bird} questions), since the test set of BIRD
is not public, and on Spider test (\fact{questions.spider} questions).
BIRD is the main dataset: it carries every baseline and every ablation
except the one with pooled clients, which is run on Spider. On Spider,
we repeat the main comparison and run the further federated setups of
RQ2 and the experiments of RQ3. Results are also reported by difficulty
level: simple, moderate or challenging in BIRD dev, and easy, medium,
hard or extra hard in Spider, as assigned by its evaluation script.

For RQ3, we add Spider dev (\fact{questions.spiderdev} questions) and
three perturbed sets derived from it,
Spider-Syn~\cite{gan2021spidersyn} (\fact{questions.syn}),
Spider-Realistic~\cite{deng2021realistic} (\fact{questions.realistic})
and Spider-DK~\cite{gan2021spiderdk} (\fact{questions.dk}).

Execution accuracy (EX) counts a prediction as correct when its
execution result equals that of the gold SQL query on the database of
the question. The query is read from the output of the model in three
steps, in this order: the content of a fenced code block is taken if the
output has one, and otherwise the whole output; this text is cut at its
first semicolon; and whatever precedes the first \texttt{SELECT} is
dropped, unless the text begins with \texttt{WITH}.

On BIRD, the two execution results are compared as sets of rows, with
one time limit of \setting{timeout.bird} for the prediction and the gold
SQL query together. On Spider, we use the test-suite evaluation
code~\cite{zhong2020testsuite}: results are equal as multisets of rows
up to a permutation of the columns, row order counts only if the gold
SQL query contains \texttt{ORDER BY}, and each query has a time limit of
\setting{timeout.spider}. A prediction that fails to execute or exceeds
the time limit is incorrect. The execution labels of
Section~\ref{sec:pref-round} follow the same steps, comparison and time
limit.

Test-suite accuracy (TS)~\cite{zhong2020testsuite} is stricter: the
prediction must also match the gold SQL query on every database of a
test suite. We report TS on Spider dev, Spider-Syn and Spider-Realistic,
for which test suites exist.

\subsection{Federated setups}\label{sec:fed-setups}

We simulate the clients by assigning each database of a training set,
with all its questions, to one client. The clients thus share no
database and differ in domain and schema in every setup, the IID one
included. What carries over to the evaluation databases, which are new
to every client, is the structure of the SQL queries; the non-IID setups
therefore skew the clients by query complexity.

The complexity of a question is the hardness level of its gold SQL query
under the criteria of Spider. For Spider train, the evaluation script of
Spider gives it. Its parser cannot read most queries of BIRD train,
which has no levels, so there we apply the criteria to each query as
parsed by a general SQL parser (Appendix~\ref{app:settings}). Each
database falls into one of four tiers, the quartiles of its share of
hard and extra hard questions. The tiers are distributed one after
another, as in the label-distribution skew of
NIID-Bench~\cite{li2022niidbench} with the tier as the label and a
database as the unit: the proportions of the clients are drawn from
$\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)$~\cite{hsu2019noniid}, a smaller $\alpha$ giving a
stronger skew, and the databases of the tier are assigned accordingly. A
client that holds at least $1/K$ of all databases when a tier comes up
receives none of that tier, and the partition is drawn again until every
client holds at least \fact{split.spider.a0.1.k5.min_dbs} databases
(\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k10.min_dbs} with ten clients). The IID setup
assigns each client the same number of databases at random; its clients
still differ in size, as the databases do.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

The main model is \setting{model}~\cite{hui2024qwen25coder}, a code
language model of \setting{model.size} parameters, which a client can
fine-tune and serve itself. For RQ2, we add two models,
\setting{models.other}~\cite{guo2024deepseekcoder}: the first, a smaller
model of the same family, is trained on Spider, and the second, of
another family, on Spider and on BIRD. The three models are trained with
the same settings, except for a longer limit on the training sequence
for DeepSeek-Coder (Appendix~\ref{app:settings}).

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

Every row uses the same model, prompt and decoding. The federated rows
also share the clients and the number of rounds,
$T = \setting{rounds.total}$. Two baselines are trained without
federation. In Local SFT, each client fine-tunes the model alone for
\setting{central.epochs} epochs; each of the $K$ resulting models is
evaluated on the whole evaluation set, and the row gives their mean with
the lowest and the highest client. Centralized SFT trains for
\setting{central.epochs} epochs on the union of the clients' data, which
the setting does not permit.

FedAvg SFT~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg}, the main baseline, consists of $T$
warm-up rounds (Section~\ref{sec:warmup}). \method{} differs from it
only in the last \setting{rounds.pref} rounds, which are preference
rounds. FedProx SFT~\cite{li2020fedprox} adds a proximal term, with
coefficient \setting{fedprox.mu}, to the local loss of FedAvg SFT, a
remedy for non-IID clients under the same supervision.

FedDPO is the federated DPO of OpenFedLLM~\cite{ye2024openfedllm}: each
client holds a preference dataset, the reference model is fixed for the
whole process, and the loss is the DPO loss alone. Our clients hold no
human preference data, so the preference dataset of a client consists of
the pairs of its first preference round in \method{}. FedDPO starts from
the global model after the same \setting{rounds.warmup} warm-up rounds,
which remains its reference model, and trains for \setting{rounds.pref}
rounds with the settings of the preference rounds and $\lambda = 0$.
FedDPO thus differs from \method{} in two ways: its pairs and its
reference model are not renewed, and its loss has no NLL term.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} ablates the two separately, and also trains
on the correct samples without the preference term.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}

The prompt (Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}) follows the Text-to-SQL
evaluation code released with Qwen2.5-Coder~\cite{hui2024qwen25coder} in
layout and wording: a single user message with the schema as
\texttt{CREATE TABLE} statements. We keep the two forms in which this
code renders the benchmarks: on Spider, each statement is followed by
three example rows of its table~\cite{chang2023prompt}; on BIRD, the
external knowledge provided with each question precedes the question
instead.

The LoRA adapter~\cite{hu2022lora} has rank \setting{lora.r}, and all
training uses AdamW~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw} with a cosine schedule.
The learning rate of SFT, \setting{sft.lr}, and the
\setting{central.epochs} epochs of centralized and local SFT are those
of the LoRA fine-tuning that SPFT-SQL~\cite{zhang2025spftsql} reports
for this model. The settings of the preference rounds follow
CodeLutra~\cite{tao2024codelutra}: the number of samples per question
and their temperature, $\beta$, the learning rate, \setting{pref.lr},
the \setting{pref.batch} pairs per update, the single epoch and the
weight decay. Appendix~\ref{app:settings} lists all settings.

Predictions are generated with vLLM~\cite{kwon2023vllm} by greedy
decoding, and the federation is simulated on one GPU
(\setting{hw.gpu}). All rows share one training seed, which sets the
order of the training examples and the sampling of the preference
rounds. FedAvg SFT and \method{} on BIRD are trained again with a second
seed, as two further rows of the main table of
Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Statistical test}\label{sec:stat-test}

Two rows are compared with the exact McNemar
test~\cite{mcnemar1947note,dietterich1998tests} on their paired
predictions. Each table names the row against which its differences are
tested: FedAvg SFT in Section~\ref{sec:results}, where one table also
tests \method{} against its variant without warm-up rounds, and
\method{} at the same number of rounds in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.
The two rows of the second seed are tested against each other, and the
Local SFT row, a mean over $K$ models, has no test. The tests of a table
form one family, within which we apply the Holm
correction~\cite{holm1979simple}; the comparisons quoted only in the
text form a family of their own. A difference is called significant at
the \fact{test.alpha} level after the correction.
