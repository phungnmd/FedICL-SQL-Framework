\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and metrics}\label{sec:datasets}

The clients hold the training sets of two Text-to-SQL benchmarks. On
BIRD~\cite{li2023bird}, they hold BIRD train,
\fact{split.bird.a0.1.k5.questions} questions on
\fact{split.bird.a0.1.k5.databases} databases, each question with the
external knowledge that the benchmark attaches to it. Since the test set
of BIRD is not public, we evaluate on BIRD dev, \fact{questions.bird}
questions on \fact{suite.bird.databases} databases. On
Spider~\cite{yu2018spider}, the clients hold Spider train,
\fact{split.spider.a0.1.k5.questions} questions on
\fact{split.spider.a0.1.k5.databases} databases, and we evaluate on
Spider test, \fact{questions.spider} questions.
Section~\ref{sec:perturbed} also evaluates the models trained on Spider
train on Spider dev (\fact{questions.spiderdev} questions) and on three
sets built from Spider: Spider-Syn~\cite{gan2021spidersyn}
(\fact{questions.syn}), in which the schema-related words of the
questions are replaced by synonyms, Spider-Realistic~\cite{deng2021realistic}
(\fact{questions.realistic}), from which explicit mentions of column
names are removed, and Spider-DK~\cite{gan2021spiderdk}
(\fact{questions.dk}), in which questions are modified to require
domain knowledge. In both benchmarks, the databases of the training
set and those of the evaluation sets are disjoint.

Execution accuracy (EX) counts a prediction as correct when its
execution result equals that of the gold SQL query under the comparison
of the benchmark's evaluation: equality of the sets of rows on BIRD, and
the result comparison of the test-suite evaluation
script~\cite{zhong2020testsuite} on Spider. Queries run under the time
limit of the same evaluation. On BIRD, one budget of
\setting{timeout.bird} covers a prediction followed by the gold SQL
query, which is executed with each prediction, and on Spider each query
has \setting{timeout.spider}. A prediction that reaches the limit is
incorrect. A query counts as reaching the limit only when it used the
processor for at least four fifths of the elapsed time. One that reached
it while waiting for the processor is run once more with no other query
running, and the outcome of that run counts. At
\setting{rounds.total} rounds, \fact{timeouts.bird.fedavg.4} predictions
of FedAvg SFT and \fact{timeouts.bird.k2.4} of \method{} reach the limit
on BIRD dev, and none on Spider test.

Execution labeling (Section~\ref{sec:labeling}) applies the same limits
to the training questions, and the same rule to a query that reaches
its limit. A sample that exhausts its limit while it runs is an
execution error and is labeled incorrect. The time that a completed
gold SQL query adds to the budget of a sample on BIRD is its elapsed
time, which depends on the load of the machine. The number of questions
that the time limit leaves without labels therefore differs between
rounds (Section~\ref{sec:fed-setups}). On Spider, each query
has its own limit, so a completed sample is always compared with the
gold result, and the completed samples without a label of
Section~\ref{sec:labeling} occur on BIRD alone.

Test-suite accuracy (TS)~\cite{zhong2020testsuite} counts a prediction as
correct when its result equals the gold result on the original database
and on every database of a suite of content variants. We use the
official suite of the databases of Spider dev, which also serves
Spider-Syn and Spider-Realistic, whose questions are posed on the same
databases. Spider test and Spider-DK have no suite and are reported
under EX alone.

BIRD has no official suite, and we generate one for BIRD dev:
\fact{suite.variants} variants of each of its \fact{suite.bird.databases}
databases, with the fuzzing procedure of
Zhong et al.~\cite{zhong2020testsuite} (Section~4 of their paper and the
fuzzer of their release), which we reimplement. A variant keeps the
schema of its database, and its tables are filled in foreign-key order
with random values of the column types, mixed with values of the source
database and with the literals of the gold SQL queries. Without a
distillation step, every variant is kept. A variant counts for a
question when the gold SQL query executes on it within the time limit,
returns at least one row, and has no result made ambiguous by ties under
\texttt{ORDER BY} with \texttt{LIMIT}. A prediction is correct under TS
when it is correct under EX on the original database and returns the
gold result on every variant that counts. Of the \fact{questions.bird}
questions of BIRD dev, \fact{suite.bird.counted} have at least one
variant that counts, and the other \fact{suite.bird.none} keep their EX
verdict.

On Spider dev, which has an official suite, we generated a suite in the
same way from its gold SQL queries, with the same number of variants,
and compared the verdicts of the two suites on the predictions of our
models on Spider dev (\fact{suite.calib.files} sets of predictions). Of
these predictions, \fact{suite.calib.ex_correct} are correct under EX,
and the two suites agree on \fact{suite.calib.agreement_ex_correct}\% of
them: the generated suite rejects \fact{suite.calib.recall}\% of the
\fact{suite.calib.official_rejected} that the official suite rejects,
and \fact{suite.calib.ours_only} of the
\fact{suite.calib.official_accepted} that it accepts. The number of
variants was chosen among \fact{suite.calib.candidates} as the one with
the highest agreement on these predictions, so the agreement is measured
on the data that set it. No official suite exists against which the
suite of BIRD dev itself could be compared.

\subsection{Federated setups}\label{sec:fed-setups}

We assign each database, with all its questions, to one client, so that
a client is an organization that holds its databases. The difficulty of
a training question is its level under the criteria of the evaluation
script of Spider: easy, medium, hard or extra hard. For BIRD train, we
apply the same criteria to the query as parsed by sqlglot, since the
parser of the Spider script does not read many queries of BIRD.

A partition is either independent and identically distributed (IID) or
non-IID in the difficulty of the questions. In the IID setup, the
databases are assigned at random in equal shares, so the setup is IID
over databases, not over questions. In a non-IID setup,
the databases are grouped into four tiers by the quartiles of their
share of hard and extra-hard questions. For each tier, the shares of the
clients are drawn from a Dirichlet distribution with concentration
$\alpha$, and the databases of the tier are assigned in these shares.
This is the label-distribution skew of NIID-Bench~\cite{li2022niidbench}
with the tier as label and the database as unit, including its rule
that a client that already holds its equal share of the databases when
a tier is assigned receives none of that tier. A client can thus exceed
its equal share within one tier. The partition is drawn again until every client holds at
least \fact{split.spider.a0.1.k5.min_dbs} databases
(\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k10.min_dbs} with
\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k10.clients} clients), from split seed
\setting{split.seed}.

Table~\ref{tab:setups} lists the five setups. The two main setups,
which serve Sections~\ref{sec:main} to~\ref{sec:ablation}
and~\ref{sec:cost}, divide BIRD train among
\fact{split.bird.a0.1.k5.clients} clients and Spider train among
\fact{split.spider.a0.1.k5.clients}, both with
$\alpha = {}$\fact{split.bird.a0.1.k5.alpha}. Section~\ref{sec:breadth}
adds three partitions of Spider train:
$\alpha = {}$\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k5.alpha} with
\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k5.clients} clients, the IID setup with
\fact{split.spider.iid.k5.clients} clients, and
$\alpha = {}$\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k10.alpha} with
\fact{split.spider.a0.5.k10.clients} clients.

Some training questions yield no preference pair whatever the model
samples (Section~\ref{sec:pairs}). Spider train has
\fact{train.spider.no_rows.databases} databases without rows, which hold
\fact{train.spider.no_rows} questions, and BIRD train has none. No
evaluation set contains such a database. The gold
SQL query fails to execute for \fact{train.bird.gold_error} questions of
BIRD train and \fact{train.spider.gold_error} of Spider train. On BIRD,
it exceeds the time limit for \fact{train.bird.gold_timeout.pref1}
questions in the first preference round of \method{} and
\fact{train.bird.gold_timeout.pref2} in the second, and on Spider for
none. All of these questions remain training targets in the warm-up
rounds.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

All experiments use \setting{model}~\cite{hui2024qwen25coder} except
those of Section~\ref{sec:breadth}, which adds
\fact{model.qwen05}-Instruct and
\fact{model.dsc13}-Instruct~\cite{guo2024deepseekcoder}. Every model thus has
\setting{models.size.min} to \setting{model.size} parameters and is
trained with a LoRA adapter, and no larger model is evaluated. Each
model receives the prompt in its own chat format
(Appendix~\ref{app:prompt}). The limit on a training sequence is
\setting{max_len.deepseek} for DeepSeek-Coder, whose tokenizer gives
longer sequences, and otherwise \setting{max_len}.

\subsection{Baselines}\label{sec:baselines}

Every trained baseline uses the adapter configuration of \method, and
every model is evaluated with the same prompt and decoding.

\textit{No training} is the pretrained model with the prompt.
\textit{Local SFT} fine-tunes an adapter at each client on its own
training set alone, and its row gives the mean, the lowest and the
highest EX over the clients.
\textit{Centralized SFT} trains one adapter on the union of the clients' training sets for
\setting{central.epochs} epochs. It pools the data that federated
training keeps apart and serves as a reference, not as a federated
baseline.

\textit{FedAvg SFT}~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg,ye2024openfedllm}
(Section~\ref{sec:formulation}) runs \setting{rounds.total} rounds of
\setting{sft.epochs} local epoch. Its first \setting{rounds.warmup}
rounds are the warm-up rounds of \method: \method{} continues from the
FedAvg SFT model of round \setting{rounds.warmup}, so the two share that
model exactly. On Spider, FedAvg SFT also runs a fifth round, which
gives the baseline more GPU time than \method{} uses
(Section~\ref{sec:cost}). \textit{FedProx SFT}~\cite{li2020fedprox} adds
a proximal term to the local objective of FedAvg SFT.

\textit{FedDPO}~\cite{ye2024openfedllm} trains each client in every round on one fixed preference dataset, with
one fixed reference model and the DPO loss alone. The clients hold no
preference dataset, and we give FedDPO the execution-labeled pairs of
the first preference round of \method. FedDPO starts from the same
warm-up model, with the same samples, execution labels and $M$, keeps
these pairs and this reference model in all its rounds, and has no NLL
term. Its other settings are those of the preference rounds of \method.
Its first round is therefore the first preference round of \method{}
without the NLL term.

\subsection{Implementation}\label{sec:implementation}

Evaluation and execution labeling read the SQL query of an output of the
model in the same way. If the output contains a fenced code block, its
body is taken. The text is then cut at the first semicolon, and what
precedes the first \texttt{SELECT} is dropped, except that a query
beginning with \texttt{WITH} keeps its \texttt{WITH} clause. An output
without \texttt{SELECT} is executed as it is, and a sample of which
nothing remains contains no query in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:labeling}. Every database is opened read-only, so a
statement that writes fails and no output of the model can change a
database.

The adapter is LoRA of rank \setting{lora.r} with \texttt{lora\_alpha}
\setting{lora.alpha} on all attention and feed-forward projection
matrices (\setting{lora.modules}). All clients start from one shared
initialization, and the server averages the two LoRA factors of each
matrix separately, as element-wise weighted means, not their product.
Warm-up rounds and FedAvg SFT rounds train with
\setting{sft.optimizer}~\cite{loshchilov2019adamw}, a learning rate of
\setting{sft.lr}, \setting{sft.batch} examples per update, a
learning-rate warm-up over \setting{sft.warmup} and LoRA dropout
\setting{sft.dropout}. Preference rounds train with
\setting{pref.optimizer}, a learning rate of \setting{pref.lr},
\setting{pref.batch} pairs per update, a learning-rate warm-up over
\setting{pref.warmup} and LoRA dropout \setting{pref.dropout}. The
schedule, like the optimizer, starts anew at every client in every
round. The number of samples per question, the sampling temperature,
$\beta$, the learning rate, the number of pairs per update, the number
of epochs and the weight decay of the preference rounds follow
CodeLutra~\cite{tao2024codelutra}, which constructs ten pairs per
question where we construct \setting{pref.pairs}.

Sampling and evaluation run on vLLM~\cite{kwon2023vllm}. At evaluation,
the model decodes greedily, with one output per question, at most
\setting{eval.tokens} new tokens and no repetition penalty. Each trained
model comes from one training run, with seed \setting{seed} except in
the rows of Table~\ref{tab:main} marked as a second seed. The seed sets
\setting{seed.role}. All runs use one type of GPU, the \setting{hw.gpu}, and train the
clients of a round one after another. The frozen model is held in
bfloat16, and the adapter is stored and communicated in 32-bit floats.
Appendix~\ref{app:settings} lists every setting
(Tables~\ref{tab:settings} and~\ref{tab:settings-2}) and the software
versions.

\subsection{Statistical test}\label{sec:stat-test}

We compare two models on the same evaluation set with the exact McNemar
test~\cite{mcnemar1947note,dietterich1998tests} on their paired verdicts
per question: a two-sided binomial test on the questions that exactly
one of the two models answers correctly. Within each family of tests,
the $p$-values are adjusted by the Holm correction~\cite{holm1979simple}.
The tests printed in one table form a family, a test printed in several
tables belongs to the family of the first, and the comparisons quoted
only in the text form one more family. A difference is significant when
its adjusted $p$ is below \fact{test.alpha}. The tables print the
unadjusted $p$ of each test and mark a significant difference with an
asterisk, and a $p$ quoted in the text is the adjusted one. We apply
the same test to two other paired verdicts per question, whether the
prediction ends in an execution error and whether it returns an
incorrect result (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}). Since each row is one training run, the test covers the
variation over evaluation questions, not the variation between training
runs.
