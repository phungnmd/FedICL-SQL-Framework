# Review of `review_section1.pdf` (Abstract and Section 1, Introduction)

**Where I stopped:** I read to line 110, the last item in the contributions list. That is the end of the file. There were no earlier-part files to use as context.

## 1. Findings, most important first

**F1. Major: the method's novelty is defined against a weak comparison.** Lines 48–52 and 73–75 ("trains on static external response pairs with one fixed reference model … which FedDPO lacks").
- The fixed pairs and fixed reference belong to the dataset and setup FedDPO [10] was used with. They are not limits of federated DPO as an algorithm. Running FedDPO on pairs each client builds and labels by execution is the obvious baseline.
- Several pieces are already known in centralized settings: iterative DPO with a new reference model each iteration, DPO plus NLL on the preferred sample, and execution-labelled preference pairs for Text-to-SQL. The last is the paper's own [12–15]. None of these is credited at the point where the components are presented as new.
- As a reader I was left with the question: is the contribution "centralized execution-based iterative DPO, run per client and then FedAvg'd"? The introduction never says what is new beyond that.
- Line 94–96 claims "the first federated training method for Text-to-SQL that exploits it". That needs a sharper boundary. I did not check the literature.

**F2. Major: the gain may come from execution matches that are right by coincidence, and the paper's own figure shows one.**
- In Fig. 1, SQL 1 is `COUNT(*)` while the gold query is `COUNT(name)`. It is labelled correct and used as the preferred query in both pairs. It matches only because `name` has no NULLs in this database instance. So matching on one database instance rewards queries that do not mean the same thing as the gold.
- Contribution 3 (lines 105–107) then says "a large part of the net gain comes from increased correct predictions that read tables or columns other than those of the gold SQL query". I read that as a warning sign, not as a contribution. The text gives no interpretation of whether this is good or bad.
- The qualifier "whether scored by EX or by TS on the generated suite" depends on how the test suite was generated, and that is not described.

**F3. Major: the statistics cover only test-set sampling, not training variance.** Lines 83–85, "exact McNemar test on paired single-run predictions".
- McNemar on one training run per method says nothing about variation across seeds or across Dirichlet splits. There is one split (α = 0.1) and one model.
- The very small p-values (10⁻¹¹, 10⁻²²) therefore overstate how reliable the method comparison is.

**F4. Major: the comparison is "at equal rounds" (line 102), but compute is not equal.**
- Each preference round adds N = 10 samples per question plus their execution. "Unchanged communication cost" (abstract line 22) is true, but the per-client compute is much higher.
- The intro offers no baseline that spends the same compute. Examples would be more SFT epochs, or simply fixing execution errors at inference time with execution feedback or retries.

**F5. Major: most of the gain is removed execution errors.** On BIRD, 140 errors disappear: about 95 become correct and 45 become wrong results (613 → 658). The Spider numbers follow the same pattern.
- That raises the question of whether a cheap inference-time execution filter would recover most of the gain without any training. The introduction does not mention this alternative.

**F6. Major/minor: round count and hyperparameter choices may have been made on the evaluation sets.**
- The baseline's "peak at 43.68%" (line 86) is picked across rounds on BIRD dev, which also serves as the test set.
- The schedule (2 warm-up + 2 preference rounds), the 4-round budget, and N = 10, M = 2 are given with no statement of how they were chosen.
- FedAvg SFT is reported at round 5 (73.92%), but FedExec-SQL is not. That makes me wonder whether 4 rounds was a favourable cut-off.

**F7. Minor: how pairs are built is not clear, even from the figure's example.**
- Fig. 1 has 7 correct and 3 incorrect samples, yet M = 2 pairs, and both use SQL 1 as the preferred query. Is the preferred query fixed? Are the rejected ones split by type of error? Is the choice random?
- The NLL term is "on correct samples" (abstract line 15), "on the correct sample" (line 75), and "weighted" only in line 74. It is unclear whether it ever applies to the gold SQL.
- Questions where all 10 samples are correct also produce no pair. Only the all-incorrect case is counted (lines 66–67).

**F8. Minor: the "two reasons" paragraph has a logic slip.** Lines 42–52: the second reason (FedDPO uses static external pairs) does not explain why client databases go unused. FedDPO was never applied to Text-to-SQL, so the point says nothing about databases.

**F9. Minor: the abstract presents teacher forcing as a defect, at length.** Lines 4–7: "the model generates no queries so no loss term is computed on its own outputs … training never identifies which of the model's queries are incorrect". This describes how SFT works by design, and it takes three clauses that are repeated almost word for word in lines 44–48.

**F10. Remark: privacy and leakage statements.**
- "All private data stay within the client boundary" (line 61) is true at the level of raw data. But the per-round integer weight, if it is a pair count, reveals how well the global model does on each client's data in each round.
- Adapter leakage is not discussed. This is standard for FL papers, but it is worth one sentence.
