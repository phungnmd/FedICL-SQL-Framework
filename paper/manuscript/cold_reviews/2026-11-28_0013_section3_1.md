## 1. Findings (most important first)

**F1. Execution match is treated as ground truth, but it can mark wrong queries as correct.** *Major.*
- **Where:** §3.2, "comparing exec(y,d) with exec(y⋆,d) partitions the queries … into correct and incorrect ones"; §3.5, lines 68–71; §3.6, lines 86–88.
- **What happened to me:** The only failure case the text admits is the extreme one: databases with empty tables. A wrong query can still return the same result as the gold query on the particular database contents. That is the reason test-suite evaluation exists for Spider. Here, such a query becomes y⁺, and Eq. (4) then raises its likelihood twice: once through the preference term and once through the NLL term. I expected a sentence on how often this happens, or on why it is tolerable. The empty-table exclusion shows the authors know about the problem but handle only its limiting case.

**F2. The hardest questions get no training signal at all in the preference rounds.** *Major.*
- **Where:** §3.4, "After 2 warm-up rounds, 2,698 questions remain without a correct sample"; §3.6, lines 85–86; Eq. (4).
- **What happened to me:** In a preference round the NLL term is computed on a sampled y⁺, never on the gold query. So 28.6% of BIRD questions (2,698 of 9,428) are dropped from training in rounds 3–4. FedAvg SFT keeps training on these questions. The text never states this consequence. I worked it out from three separate places. The ablation pointer ("gold SQL query in place of a missing correct sample") shows the authors are aware of the option, but Section 3 should state the cost directly.

**F3. The pair count that is uploaded reveals information derived from execution labels.** *Major, because privacy is the stated premise of the paper.*
- **Where:** §3.7, "A client thus transmits its adapter (73.9 MB) and one integer, exactly as in a warm-up round"; "Samples, execution results, execution labels and preference pairs remain at the client"; Fig. 1, "Only adapters and pair counts travel".
- **What happened to me:**
  - In warm-up, n_k is a constant. It could be sent once and never needs to be sent every round.
  - |P_k^t| changes every round. It equals M × (the number of the client's questions on which the global model is partly right), so the server learns a per-round accuracy statistic for each client.
  - A client with empty P sends nothing, and the server learns that as well.
  - "Exactly as in a warm-up round" is true of the message size, not of what the message reveals. The claim that labels stay at the client needs that qualification.

**F4. Weighting clients by pair count is not justified.** *Minor.*
- **Where:** §3.7, line 114, "by Eq. (2) with |P_k^t| in place of n_k"; Algorithm 1, line 16.
- **What happened to me:** I looked for a rationale and found only "Section 5.3 examines the choice". The weighting down-weights any client whose data is mostly too hard or mostly too easy for the current global model. Under non-IID partitions (Table 1: hard-or-extra share of 20–60% per client), those are exactly the clients that differ most. A sentence on why this is the right estimator, rather than n_k, is missing.

**F5. The labeling rules contradict each other and depart from the metric they claim to follow.** *Minor.*
- **Where:** §3.5, lines 70–75.
- **What happened to me:**
  - Line 70 says a sample that "exceeds the time limit" is incorrect.
  - Lines 73–75 say that on BIRD, where "a sample and the gold SQL query share one time limit", a sample whose comparison does not finish within the limit is unlabeled.
  - If the two queries share one limit, I could not tell how a slow sample (labeled incorrect) is separated from a slow comparison (unlabeled).
  - Line 69 says labels follow "the comparison of the evaluation metric". As far as I know, BIRD's official evaluation scores a timeout as incorrect, so the training label departs from the metric on exactly this case.
  - The empty-table exclusion in §3.6 is a fourth no-label/no-pair case, but §3.5 announces "Three cases".

**F6. The pair-drawing procedure is underspecified.** *Minor.*
- **Where:** §3.6, lines 83–85, "drawn uniformly and independently, without replacement when … and with replacement otherwise".
- **What happened to me:**
  - "Independently" and "without replacement" do not fit together, and I had to reread the sentence.
  - With M = 2, a question with exactly one correct and one incorrect sample produces the same pair twice, so it gets double weight in the loss.
  - Nothing says whether identical sample strings are removed first. At temperature 1 with N = 10, duplicates are likely for easy questions.

**F7. The statistics in §3.2 and §3.4 have no stated source.** *Minor.*
- **Where:** 62.3%, 4,035 and 2,698 (§3.2, §3.4); and "which already yields preference pairs" (lines 60–61).
- **What happened to me:**
  - These numbers come from one stochastic draw of 10 samples at temperature 1. The text does not say which partition, which seed, or whether questions with a failing gold query are in the denominator.
  - The model "after 2 rounds of FedAvg SFT" depends on the partition, and Table 1 lists several partitions.
  - The T_w = 0 claim needs the number of pairs it produces. From the text I can only bound it: at most 9,428 − 4,035 questions.

**F8. Algorithm 1, Figure 1 and the text disagree on details.** *Minor.* Details are in §4 of this report.

**F9. The novelty is framed only against FedDPO.** *Remark.* Section 2 is not yet written, so this may be addressed there.
- **Where:** Opening paragraph (lines 7–10); §3.6; §3.7, lines 103–104 and 112.
- **What happened to me:** Each of the three stated "departures" is a standard feature of iterative preference optimization in the centralized setting: resampling every iteration, reference model = the previous iterate, and an NLL term on the winning sample (e.g. iterative RPO, Pang et al. 2024). Section 3 contrasts only with FedDPO, so I could not tell which part is the contribution and which is inherited from [5,6]. Separately, [3] is cited for both FedAvg SFT and FedDPO, which made me unsure what [3] is.

**F10. Two sentences claim more than they can, or say nothing.** *Remark.*
- Lines 27–28: "the model receives no signal about which of its own queries are incorrect". This is too strong. NLL under softmax lowers the probability mass of all non-gold sequences; what is missing is a *targeted* signal.
- Lines 76–77: "an incorrect query appears among the samples with the probability that the current global model assigns to it". This is either a tautology or wrong: with N samples the probability of appearing is 1 − (1 − p)^N, not p.
