## Response

# Referee report

**Manuscript:** "FedLS-SQL: title to be set" (Computers, Materials & Continua, doi placeholder 10.32604/cmc.2026.012345)

---

## 1. Summary

The paper looks at federated fine-tuning of a small open model for Text-to-SQL. Several organizations each hold questions, gold SQL and their own databases, and they cannot pool them. The standard method is FedAvg with LoRA and supervised fine-tuning (FedAvg SFT). It trains only on the gold query and never runs anything against the clients' databases. The authors argue that execution on those databases is a supervision signal that only the clients can produce.

FedLS-SQL works as follows:

- **Warm-up:** T_w = 2 rounds of FedAvg SFT.
- **Preference rounds:** T − T_w = 2 rounds. In each one, every client:
  - samples N = 10 queries per question from the current global model;
  - executes them on its own databases and labels each correct or incorrect against the gold result;
  - builds M = 2 (correct, incorrect) pairs for every question that has both labels;
  - trains on DPO + λ·token-averaged NLL on the correct sample, using the round's global model as the DPO reference.
- **Aggregation:** the server averages the adapters, weighted by each client's pair count.

So the pairs and the reference model are renewed every round, which is what separates it from FedDPO in OpenFedLLM.

The main setup has 5 clients, a database-level Dirichlet skew on query hardness (α = 0.1) and Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct. After 4 rounds, FedLS-SQL reaches:

| Benchmark | FedLS-SQL | FedAvg SFT | Gain | McNemar p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BIRD dev | 48.76 | 42.57 | +6.19 | 4.6e-11 |
| Spider test | 78.20 | 74.71 | +3.49 | 6.4e-7 |

Execution errors roughly halve; incorrect-result predictions do not fall. A preference round costs 0.9–2.7× the client time of an SFT round, and communication is unchanged. Ablations cover:

- fixed pairs and reference model;
- no NLL term;
- no preference term;
- the number of warm-up rounds;
- the aggregation weights.

## 2. Contribution and novelty

Each part of the method is known:

- execution-labelled preference pairs for SQL (Liu et al. 2025, ExCoT);
- iterative on-policy DPO + NLL with the previous model as reference (Pang et al., Iterative RPO; CodeLutra, which already evaluates on Text-to-SQL);
- FedAvg over LoRA adapters (OpenFedLLM, FederatedScope-LLM);
- federated DPO (FedDPO).

The contribution is to combine them in the federated Text-to-SQL setting. Each FL round becomes one iteration of CodeLutra-style training, with labels computed where the databases are.

The authors argue that execution is only available at the clients, which is correct and is a useful framing. It reads as a natural observation rather than a technical advance, though. Nothing in the algorithm is federated-specific beyond:

- weighting by pair count, which the ablation shows makes no difference;
- using the global model as the reference, which is what any iterative DPO would do at the start of an iteration.

The claim to be "the first method for the federated training of Text-to-SQL that uses this supervision" (l. 86) is plausible. My brief search found no prior work that combines federated training, Text-to-SQL and execution-labelled on-policy pairs. The novelty is incremental, however, and the paper's value rests almost entirely on how complete and convincing the empirical study is.

Some related work is missing and should be discussed:

- **Centralized execution-driven self-improvement for Text-to-SQL that uses rejection sampling plus preference training,** e.g. ExeSQL. This is closer to FedLS-SQL's local step than Liu et al.
- **Self-training / rejection-sampling fine-tuning** (STaR, RFT, ReST). This is the obvious non-preference way to use execution labels (see Major issue 3).
- **Recent federated DPO variants,** including FedPDPO and federated preference alignment that builds pairs from on-policy generations. These weaken the contrast in Table 1, which presents FedDPO as the only federated preference baseline.
- **Fed-GRPO and PubSwap** are cited (refs 24–25) as federated RL with verifiable rewards but are not compared against. Federated GRPO with an execution reward is a direct competitor that uses the same signal. The paper should at least argue why DPO pairs were chosen over it.

## 3. Soundness of method and experiments

**Method.** The method is clearly specified and sensible. Algorithm 1, the pairing rule, the treatment of truncated samples, databases without rows and failing gold queries are all described carefully. The NLL term is well motivated (l. 238–241).

I checked a number of reported quantities for internal consistency, and they agree:

- the discordant counts (153 vs 58 → 95/1534 = 6.19%);
- incorrect-prediction totals vs EX (e.g. 658 + 128 = 786 = 1534 × (1 − 0.4876));
- the pair counts (= 2 × questions);
- round times and ratios in Table 7;
- the 73.9 MB adapter size, which matches rank-16 LoRA on all projections of Qwen2.5-1.5B in fp32.

Spider round-1 question accounting closes exactly (4,013 + 2,327 + 557 + 3 = 6,900). BIRD round-1 accounting leaves about 87 questions unexplained (5,877 + 416 + 2,698 + 350 = 9,341 of 9,428); presumably these are questions whose samples were all truncated or that sit on databases without rows. This should be stated.

**Experiments.** The setup has good features:

- exact McNemar tests with Holm correction;
- per-difficulty breakdowns;
- test-suite accuracy;
- the perturbed Spider sets;
- the split into execution errors vs incorrect results;
- the analysis of whether correct predictions use the gold tables and columns;
- honest reporting of non-significant results.

But as it stands, the evidence supports only one claim: on the one main setup, with one seed and one model family, FedLS-SQL beats FedAvg SFT at 4 rounds on BIRD dev and Spider test. Most of the other claims have no numbers behind them (Major issue 1), and the main comparison is not controlled for compute, optimizer settings, or the obvious self-training alternative (Major issues 2–3).

## 4. Clarity and presentation

The prose is precise but unusually hard to read. Sentences are long, nested and heavily qualified, with idioms such as "the one gold SQL query of a question" and "pairs correct with incorrect samples". Results are often stated as chains of numbers in running text rather than tables (e.g. §5.4, §6, §7.1). Section 7.1 mostly repeats §5.4 and §6. The paper would be much shorter and clearer if numbers were moved into tables and the text stated each conclusion once.

The acknowledgment discloses use of an LLM for drafting. That is appropriate, but the authors should make sure the final text is edited for readability.

Front matter and back matter are placeholders: the title ("title to be set"), the author and affiliation, and the Funding, Author Contributions, Data Availability and Conflicts of Interest statements ("To be completed"). The code release promised in l. 92 has no link.

## 5. Major issues

**1. The manuscript is incomplete; many reported experiments have no results.**

Tables 3, 4, 5 and 8 and the text are full of "–" and "??" entries. They include:

- Local SFT and Centralized SFT on BIRD;
- FedProx SFT (its proximal coefficient is also "??", l. 336);
- FedDPO, the main published federated preference baseline;
- the second-seed rows (l. 379–380);
- DeepSeek-Coder-1.3B on both benchmarks;
- three of the five federated setups (α = 0.5, IID, 10 clients);
- "Without the preference term", "No warm-up" and "Three warm-up rounds" at 4 rounds on BIRD;
- "Without the NLL term" on BIRD;
- "Clients pooled".

As a result, RQ2 (other models and federated setups) is answered only for Qwen-0.5B on Spider. The motivation that federation beats training alone (Local SFT) is untested. Whether federation costs anything relative to pooling (Clients pooled) is unknown. The comparison with FedDPO, the only external federated preference method, is missing. The contributions list and abstract describe a study the manuscript does not contain.

*Resolution:* fill every cell or remove the rows and the claims built on them. A paper cannot be judged on results that do not exist.

**2. The comparison with FedAvg SFT does not control for optimizer settings or compute.**

The preference rounds use a different optimizer configuration from the SFT rounds:

| Setting | Preference rounds | SFT rounds |
|---|---|---|
| Learning rate | 5e-5 | 2e-4 |
| Weight decay | 1e-4 | 0.01 |
| LoRA dropout | 0 | 0.05 |
| Warm-up | 0.1 | 0.03 |

FedAvg SFT peaks at round 3 and declines at rounds 4–5. That pattern is consistent with overfitting from repeated high-learning-rate epochs over the same data, not with SFT having run out of signal. Part of the gain may therefore come from switching to a gentler optimizer in rounds 3–4.

Preference rounds also use up to 2.7× the client compute and sample 10 outputs per question. The Spider 5-round FedAvg check partly addresses compute; nothing comparable exists for BIRD.

*Resolution:* add a FedAvg SFT baseline whose rounds 3–4 use the preference-round optimizer settings (or a tuned lower learning rate). Add a compute-matched comparison on BIRD as well. Report FedAvg SFT tuned on a held-out split, not just with the hyperparameters borrowed from SPFT-SQL for centralized 3-epoch training.

**3. The paper lacks a self-training (rejection-sampling) baseline, and the preference term's contribution to EX is not established.**

The simplest way to use execution labels in FL is to sample, keep the correct samples, and fine-tune on them (federated RFT/STaR). The "Without the preference term" ablation is a restricted version of this: it applies NLL only to the correct side of pairs, and only for questions with both labels. Even so, after one round it is not significantly below FedLS-SQL in EX (−1.83 on BIRD, adjusted p = 0.46; −1.59 on Spider, adjusted p = 0.14). The authors acknowledge this (l. 509–510, 559–562).

The evidence that the DPO term matters therefore comes mainly from execution-error counts, and on BIRD the paper itself says part of that reduction is a shift to silently wrong results (l. 555–557). This matters both for the method's identity, since "preference optimization" is in the keywords and contributions, and for practice, where silent wrong answers can be worse than errors.

*Resolution:*

- Add a full federated RFT baseline that trains NLL on all correct samples of all questions, including all-correct questions, for 2 rounds after warm-up.
- Run the "without preference term" ablation at 4 rounds on both benchmarks.
- Consider a federated GRPO/execution-reward baseline (cf. Fed-GRPO).
- If the preference term does not significantly add EX over self-training, state the contribution accordingly.

**4. Variance from training is not measured.**

Every row uses one training seed and one client partition. McNemar captures only the sampling variance of the evaluation questions. It does not capture variance from adapter initialization, data order, sampling of the preference rounds, or the Dirichlet partition. The ±1–3-point effects in §5.3, Table 6 and Table 8 could easily fall within seed-to-seed variation for DPO-style training. The second-seed rows that would address this are blank.

*Resolution:* run at least 3 training seeds, and ideally several partition seeds, for the main methods and key ablations. Report mean ± std, and test across seeds (e.g. a paired test over seeds, or a mixed model) in addition to McNemar.

**5. The federated setting is narrow, and the evaluation does not match the stated use case.**

The setting has:

- 5 clients (10 in one setup with no results);
- full participation;
- 4 rounds in total;
- clients carved out of a single benchmark;
- skew only on query hardness.

This is a short, near-centralized regime in which client drift is limited, so "federated" adds little difficulty. The motivation (l. 24–29) also describes organizations wanting a model that serves their own and others' databases. Yet evaluation uses only databases that no client holds, so the setting never measures performance on clients' own held-out questions.

*Resolution:*

- Add more clients with partial participation and more rounds, including more preference rounds (the method "is not trained further", l. 585–586).
- Add a heterogeneity that matters for this method. One example: clients whose databases differ in size or in whether they contain rows, since execution labelling depends on that.
- Report per-client in-domain held-out accuracy alongside cross-domain accuracy.

**6. It is not clear how the hyperparameters and design choices were selected.**

The values T = 4, T_w = 2, M = 2, N = 10, β and λ, and the round at which results are reported, all appear to have been chosen with access to BIRD dev, the only BIRD evaluation set. Several ablations are also run on BIRD dev and Spider test.

*Resolution:* state explicitly which choices were made before seeing evaluation results. Ideally, tune on a held-out slice of the training databases, and report sensitivity to M, N, β and λ.

**7. The "Holm family" definitions are underspecified, and some significance language is loose.**

"The comparisons quoted only in the text form a family of their own" (l. 368–369) does not say which comparisons are in that family, so the corrections cannot be checked. Elsewhere, significance claims depend on which family a test sits in. For example, the incorrect-result count difference on BIRD (p = 0.007) is significant in §6 but not in §5.4 (adjusted 0.077).

*Resolution:* list every family and its members in an appendix table, with raw and adjusted p-values.

**8. The robustness and warm-up claims are weaker than they are presented.**

- **Robustness (RQ3).** FedLS-SQL is significantly better than FedAvg SFT on only 1 of 4 perturbed or dev sets in EX, and 1 of 3 in TS.
- **Warm-up.** The case for warm-up rests on a TS difference that is significant only on Spider-Syn. The no-warm-up variant has higher EX than FedLS-SQL on Spider-DK (+3.55) and level EX on Spider test, and its BIRD results are missing.

*Resolution:* soften the abstract and contributions. Alternatively, supply the missing BIRD warm-up ablations and multi-seed results to back the claims.

## 6. Minor issues

1. "FedLS-SQL" is never expanded.
2. Table 3 marks FedLS-SQL in bold as the "best federated method", but every other federated row is empty.
3. The FedDPO baseline (l. 338–345) trains on FedLS-SQL's own first-round pairs. That makes it an ablation (fixed pairs + reference, no NLL), not an independent implementation of published FedDPO. It should be labelled as such, or FedDPO should be implemented with its own pair construction.
4. In §6 ("Fixed pairs and reference"), the fixed-pairs variant retrains for a second round on the same pairs, so it also sees duplicated data. Say whether this was controlled.
5. The variant that pairs gold SQL with incorrect samples (l. 533–538) is evaluated only without warm-up and after one round. Explain why, or run it in the main configuration.
6. Report the per-client pair counts and weights, as well as how many questions per client yield pairs. Clients with mostly hard queries may contribute few pairs, so pair-count weighting could under-represent them.
7. §4.1: on BIRD, execution labels compare row sets with a combined 30 s limit. Clarify how the gold-query time is handled during labelling when the gold query is slow, and how often labels time out.
8. Discuss possible contamination of Spider and BIRD in the Qwen2.5-Coder pretraining data.
9. Give the absolute BIRD dev numbers of recent small-model methods (e.g. SLM-SQL, OmniSQL at its smallest size) for context, even though they are not federated, so readers can place 48.76% relative to the state of the art.
10. l. 75–76 and l. 400: "does not improve after round 3" should be stated per dataset. On Spider, round 3 and round 4 are equal.
11. Figure 2: add the FedLS-SQL ablation curves or error bars once multi-seed results are available.
12. Table 7: add the per-client time distribution, since a federation waits for its slowest client.
13. The header has a missing space ("2026submitted").
14. Several references are arXiv/preprint entries with no venue or identifier (10, 24, 25, 33, 34); add the arXiv IDs.
15. The privacy discussion (l. 587–590) should mention that the pair count reveals client accuracy every round. A short note on compatibility with secure aggregation would help.

## 7. Recommendation: **Reject** (resubmission encouraged)

My decision rests on the following:

- **The manuscript is incomplete.** Most baseline rows (Local SFT, Centralized SFT on BIRD, FedProx, FedDPO), the second seed, the second model family, three of five federated setups and several key ablations have no results. One hyperparameter is "??", and the title, authors and declarations are placeholders. A large share of the stated contributions and RQ2 cannot be evaluated.
- **The one complete comparison is not controlled.** FedAvg SFT is the only completed baseline. The comparison does not control for the change in optimizer settings or for compute on BIRD, and it omits the natural federated self-training baseline. The paper's own ablation already suggests that baseline would be close in EX.
- **Training variance is unmeasured.** All results come from a single training seed and a single partition.
- **The novelty is incremental.** The method combines iterative execution-labelled DPO + NLL (CodeLutra / Iterative RPO) with FedAvg-LoRA and adds no federated-specific mechanism. Its value therefore depends on a complete and rigorous empirical study, which is not yet here.

The core idea is reasonable, and the analysis is carefully done: the error decomposition, the TS checks, the cost accounting and the honest reporting of null results. The numbers that are reported are internally consistent. A complete version that addresses Major issues 1–4 could be a solid contribution to this journal, and I would be glad to see it resubmitted.
