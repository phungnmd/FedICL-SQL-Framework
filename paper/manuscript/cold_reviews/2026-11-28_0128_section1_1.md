## Response

# Reviewer report on Section 1 (Abstract and Introduction) of "FedExec-SQL"

**Where I stopped:** I read the whole part, from the title block through the last contribution bullet (p. 4, l. 105). The section ends without a paragraph describing how the paper is organised.

**Arithmetic check:** The headline numbers are consistent with each other. On BIRD dev, 1,534 − 268 − 613 = 653 correct (42.57%) and 1,534 − 128 − 658 = 748 correct (48.76%). On Spider test, 2,147 − 173 − 370 = 1,604 correct (74.71%) and 2,147 − 84 − 384 = 1,679 correct (78.20%). The differences +6.19, +3.49 and +5.08 are also correct. The 73.9 MB adapter size matches LoRA rank 16 on all linear layers of Qwen2.5-1.5B stored in fp32, but the text never states this.

## 1. Findings (most important first)

1. **Major: the motivation blames federation for something that is not caused by federation.** The text says (l. 54–56): "Both limitations share one underlying structural cause: determining whether a candidate query matches the gold SQL query requires execution on the target relational database, which data isolation confines to the client."
   - When I reached this sentence the argument stopped holding for me. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) lacks execution feedback in centralized training too. That comes from the training objective, not from data isolation.
   - FedDPO's static pairs and fixed reference model are design choices, not consequences of isolation.
   - The introduction never places the method against centralized methods that already train Text-to-SQL models with execution feedback and preference or reinforcement learning. So I could not tell what is new beyond "iterative DPO with execution labels, run inside FedAvg."
   - The claim "to our knowledge, the first federated training method for Text-to-SQL that exploits it" (l. 93–94) cannot be checked from these pages.

2. **Major: FedDPO is set up as the second limitation, but no result for it is reported.** The text says (l. 50–53): "FedDPO [10,11] optimizes static preference pairs drawn from fixed external datasets … omits NLL regularization." Then:
   - Every number in the Abstract and Introduction compares against FedAvg SFT only. I finished the section not knowing whether FedExec-SQL beats FedDPO.
   - [11] is cited again at l. 74 for "a DPO preference term [11]", which suggests [11] is the original, non-federated DPO paper. Citing it for "FedDPO" makes me unsure which federated method is being described.
   - "Omits NLL regularization" describes a design choice, not a limitation. It is phrased as a defect without evidence that it hurts.

3. **Major: the statistical evidence is not described.** The p-values at l. 83–89 (e.g. "p = 4.6 × 10⁻¹¹") come with:
   - no named test;
   - no number of training seeds or client partitions;
   - no definition of the correction family behind "neither significant after correction" (l. 90).

   If these are per-question paired tests (e.g. McNemar) on a single federated run, they ignore variance across training seeds and Dirichlet splits. With α = 0.1 and five clients, that variance is likely large. The statement "we establish statistically significant EX gains" (l. 99) rests on this.

4. **Major: the comparison is at equal communication, not equal compute, and key reference points are missing.** The text says "at equal rounds" (l. 99) and "at unchanged communication cost" (abstract, l. 20–21).
   - Per round, FedExec-SQL samples 10 queries per question, executes them, and runs DPO against a reference model. FedAvg SFT does none of this.
   - The introduction does not give: the pretrained zero-shot EX, a local-only lower bound, a centralized upper bound, or a simple control of SFT on execution-filtered self-samples. That last control would show whether the gain comes from the DPO term or just from extra correct training targets.
   - Without the zero-shot number I cannot tell how much the 4 federated rounds add at all.

5. **Minor: Figure 1 contradicts its caption and the method.**
   - The caption says "one Spider training question", but the panel shows an "Evidence" field and the database `student_loan`. The evidence field is specific to BIRD, and `student_loan` is, as far as I know, a BIRD training database.
   - The caption also says the samples come from "the pretrained model at temperature 1". In the method, preference rounds sample from the global model *after* two warm-up rounds.
   - The pretrained model gets 7 of 10 samples correct here. That weakens the stated reason for warm-up at l. 66–67 ("so that the model produces correct samples").

6. **Minor: the error analysis is worded to understate what happened.** The text says (l. 89–90): "executable queries that return an incorrect result do not decrease (613 vs. 658 and 370 vs. 384)."
   - These counts *increase*, by 45 and 14.
   - About a third of the removed execution errors on BIRD (45 of 140) became wrong results. "Do not decrease" hides this.
   - "613 vs. 658" does not say which method is which. I had to work it out from the totals.

7. **Minor: contribution 3 is opaque.** The text says (l. 100–102): "trace their origin to halved execution errors and to predictions referencing non-gold tables or columns that are correct under EX and, on BIRD dev, test-suite accuracy (TS)."
   - I could not tell whether "non-gold tables or columns" describes a source of genuine gains or a worry about spurious matches.
   - Test-suite accuracy is normally a Spider metric. Here it is applied to BIRD dev and not to Spider test, which looks reversed and is not explained.

8. **Minor: peaks are reported selectively.** The text says (l. 84–86): "FedAvg SFT stops improving after the third round on both benchmarks."
   - A peak value is given for BIRD (43.68%) but not for Spider.
   - The Abstract compares only against FedAvg SFT's final round, which is lower than its peak, so the margin looks larger than the margin over the peak.
   - With only 4 rounds, "stops improving" rests on one data point, and it may mean the baseline was not tuned (learning rate, local epochs).

9. **Major (editorial): the wrong journal template is used.** The header, DOI and copyright line read "Comput Mater Contin.", "Tech Science Press" and "10.32604/cmc.2026.012345", and the license line says "Submitted to Comput Mater Contin. for under…". This manuscript is for the Journal of Intelligent Information Systems.

10. **Minor: prior federated semantic-parsing work may be mischaracterized.** The text says (l. 43): "FedAvg SFT [7,8,10]", and [8] is the source of the task formulation (l. 39). If [8] is Zhang et al. (ACL 2023), that paper proposed its own aggregation scheme rather than plain FedAvg. If so, grouping it under FedAvg SFT misstates it, and it is an obvious baseline that goes unmentioned.

## 2. What I could not reproduce without guessing

- LoRA rank, target modules and precision. The 73.9 MB figure implies r = 16 on all linear layers in fp32, but this is not stated.
- Local epochs or steps, learning rate and batch size, for both warm-up and preference rounds.
- Sampling temperature and maximum length used during training. The caption's "temperature 1, 256 tokens" is given only for the illustration.
- How M = 2 pairs are chosen when there are several correct samples (7 in Fig. 1), and what happens to questions with no correct or no incorrect sample. How many questions actually yield pairs.
- Which correct sample the NLL term uses ("the correct sample", l. 75, singular).
- DPO β and the NLL weight.
- The execution-equivalence rule: ordered vs. unordered comparison, duplicates, float tolerance, timeouts.
- The "four-tier complexity Dirichlet allocation": what the tiers are, and what the Dirichlet is drawn over.
- Whether BIRD and Spider are trained as separate federations or one joint federation, and how much data each client holds.
- What the "integer aggregation weight" counts during preference rounds: questions, pairs or samples.
- The prompt format on BIRD (sample rows are specified only for Spider, l. 47), and the decoding used at evaluation.
- The statistical test, the correction method, and the number of seeds or splits.

## 3. What I did not believe, or found asserted without support

- "a single organization typically holds few internal databases and annotated question-SQL pairs" (l. 33–34): no evidence given.
- "no supervision penalizes incorrect queries" (abstract, l. 6; l. 49): NLL lowers the probability of alternatives indirectly. The claim is overstated.
- The structural-cause argument at l. 54–56 (see Finding 1).
- "FedDPO cannot target the evolving errors" (l. 53): asserted, and not tested in anything reported here.
- "on-policy" and "evolving errors": pairs are drawn once from the round's global model and then trained on for several local steps, and there are only 2 preference rounds. "Renewal" therefore happens once.
- Privacy is implied ("all private data stay within the client boundary") with no discussion of what adapters trained with DPO leak, and no secure aggregation or differential privacy. If the uploaded integer is a pair count, it also reveals how many questions the model gets wrong on each client.
- SLMs "of 0.5 billion to 1.5 billion parameters [6]" (l. 30): the range looks arbitrary, and only 1.5B is evaluated.
