## 1. Findings, most important first

**1. Major. §6, "Without the preference term … EX lies between that of the model before the round and that of FedLS-SQL".**
- What I took from this paragraph is that the method's defining component is not shown to matter, and the paragraph does not say so. NLL-only training on the model's own correct samples reaches 44.39 against 46.22 on BIRD and 75.87 against 77.46 on Spider. Neither difference is significant: the adjusted p-values are 0.46 and 0.14, and 0.057 on BIRD is not significant even before adjustment.
- The topic sentence ranks the variant "between" the two models. That ordering is not tested at either end.
- The paragraph then turns to execution errors, which it tests (p = 1.1 × 10⁻²⁴). The difference in the other direction goes untested: FedLS-SQL has 723 incorrect results against 605, and 385 against 365.
- So what the preference term demonstrably adds is executable SQL, not more correct SQL. That is a narrower claim than the method sections make.
- The NLL-only variant also runs at the preference-round learning rate (5 × 10⁻⁵) for one epoch. That setting was chosen for the combined loss, so this baseline may be under-trained.

**2. Major. End of §5.4, "those that read other tables or columns number 191 against 136, and 103 against 83 … EX establishes correctness on the evaluation database only."**
- When I subtracted, the net BIRD gain is 748 − 653 = 95 questions. Of these, 40 come from predictions that read the same tables and columns as the gold query, and 55 from predictions that read other tables or columns. On Spider the net gain is 75, split 55 and 20.
- So more than half of the BIRD gain falls in the group that the text itself flags as only weakly verified. The text raises the caveat and then moves on to cost without saying what follows from it.
- This connects to finding 3, but the text never makes the connection.

**3. Major. §6, warm-up paragraph, "At the same total of four rounds, EX on Spider test is level with no, two and three warm-up rounds."**
- The topic sentence points at the uninformative result. The informative one is two sentences later: without warm-up rounds, EX is above FedAvg SFT on all three sets while TS is below it on all three (67.21 against 67.99 on Spider dev).
- Read with finding 2, this suggests the preference rounds can raise EX through queries that are not semantically correct. The text calls it "a comparison of values without a test" and draws no conclusion.
- The next paragraph reports the counts that §3.3 promised (4,035 → 2,698; 1,016 → 557 → 470). It never says whether the warm-up rounds, which §2.2 lists as a design difference from CodeLutra, are justified by EX, by TS, or by neither.
- I finished the paragraph not knowing what the authors conclude about warm-up rounds.

**4. Minor (major if the paper claims cost-effectiveness). End of §5.4, "The fifth round of FedAvg SFT on Spider allows a comparison at more client time".**
- A compute-matched comparison exists only on Spider, where the cost ratio is lowest.
- On BIRD, the main dataset, four rounds of FedLS-SQL take 2×173 + 470 + 391 = 1,207 client-minutes against 692 for FedAvg SFT, 1.74 times as long. No matched baseline is given there, and the text does not state the totals. I computed them myself.
- On Spider, the comparison point (round 5, 73.92) is below FedAvg SFT's best (74.71, rounds 3–4). The 4.28-point margin is therefore larger than the 3.49-point margin already reported, and the extra round shows nothing beyond the plateau already visible in Fig. 2.
- "Client time" sums the clients run one after another. In a real federation, the time a round takes is set by the slowest client, and client sizes range from 683 to 3,059 questions (Table 2). The text does not mention this.

**5. Minor. §6, "Fixing both together, the row does not show from which of the two a difference arises."**
- The confound is acknowledged, but it conflicts with §2.2: "Section 6 varies all three of these choices" (pairs built once, DPO loss alone, fixed reference). Pairs and reference are never varied separately.
- A more telling fact goes unstated: "Fixed pairs and reference" (46.15) is slightly below FedLS-SQL after three rounds (46.22). A second round on reused pairs adds nothing on BIRD. The text compares the row only with four-round FedLS-SQL.

**6. Minor. §6, "An alternative, constructing M pairs … from the gold SQL query and an incorrect sample".**
- This experiment runs in a configuration not used anywhere else: no warm-up rounds, one round, BIRD only.
- Its reference value of 41.85 appears in no table, and its own row is not in Table 8.
- No conclusion is stated.
- The colon in "not significant (adjusted p = 0.058): the execution errors fall…" reads as if the error breakdown explained the non-significance.
- It is unclear whether "an incorrect sample" means one sample reused for all M pairs or M different samples.

**7. Minor. §6, "The weights of the server average lead to no significant difference in EX. The adapters of the first preference round are averaged again, without further training."**
- The test re-averages one round's adapters after the fact. The opening claim covers aggregation weights in general.
- Over several rounds, the weights would change the global model that the clients then sample from. That effect is not tested.

**8. Minor. Reporting of p-values across §6.**
- The opening says "Significance is stated after the correction of Section 4.6". The text then quotes unadjusted p-values (5.7 × 10⁻⁴, 7.6 × 10⁻⁹, 0.0014, 0.09) next to adjusted ones (0.46, 0.14, 0.19, 0.058). Only the adjusted ones are labelled.
- The adjusted values appear in no table.
- From 0.015 → 0.14 and 0.057 → 0.46, I infer a Holm family of 11 comparisons. Table 8 has 17 cells for comparisons, so these adjusted values will change when the missing cells are filled.
- "Level" and "do not differ" are used for non-significant differences, which is not the same as equivalence. The text itself notes that on Spider the second round adds only 0.74 points, so the upper-block comparisons there have little room to show a difference.

**9. Remark. §5.4 end, "as more questions have only correct samples: 416 and then 3,016 on BIRD".**
- The number of training questions with only correct samples rises seven-fold after one preference round, while BIRD dev gains 2.54 points.
- That looks like lower sample diversity on questions the model has just trained on, not only a gain in ability. The text treats it purely as a reason training gets cheaper.
- It also means the training signal shrinks from round to round, which bears on any claim about running more preference rounds.

**10. Remark. Structure.**
- The text moves between the blocks of Table 8 in this order: upper, lower, lower, upper, lower, upper.
- There is no opening statement of which design choices §6 ablates or why. §3.4 lists them, but §6 does not refer back to that list.

## 3. What I did not believe, or found asserted without support

- **"plausibly because the databases of BIRD are far larger":** this could be measured, for example per-query execution times and timeout counts. The time limits also differ (30 s against 60 s), which the text does not mention.
- **"EX lies between …":** see finding 1.
- **"The weights of the server average lead to no significant difference":** stated more generally than the test supports (finding 7).
- **"Without the NLL term, a preference round brings no gain":** the direction is plausible and the literature cited agrees, but β and the learning rate were taken from a method that uses both terms. A DPO-only run might need different settings.
- **"it shows whether the preference rounds lose by running at five clients with a server average":** pooled training also changes how batches are composed and how many optimizer steps are taken per update. The row confounds these with federation.
- **The cost argument:** it rests on one Spider comparison against a declining round (finding 4).
