## Response

# Referee report

**Manuscript:** "FedLS-SQL: title to be set," submitted to *Computers, Materials & Continua*

---

## 1. Summary

The paper looks at federated fine-tuning of a small Text-to-SQL model. Several organizations each hold questions, gold SQL and their own databases, and none can pool them. The usual approach is FedAvg with LoRA and supervised fine-tuning (FedAvg SFT). It learns only from the gold query and never runs anything on the clients' databases.

The proposed method, FedLS-SQL, starts with T_w = 2 warm-up rounds of FedAvg SFT. Then come T − T_w = 2 "preference rounds." In each one, every client:
- samples N = 10 queries per question from the current global model;
- runs them on its own database and labels each correct or incorrect by comparing its result with the gold query's result;
- builds M = 2 (correct, incorrect) pairs for every question that has both labels;
- trains its LoRA adapter for one epoch on a DPO loss plus a length-normalized NLL term on the correct sample.

The reference model is the global model at the start of the round. The server averages the adapters, weighted by each client's pair count. Communication is the same as FedAvg SFT; the extra cost is client computation.

Experiments use Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct with five simulated clients. Each training database goes to exactly one client, and clients are skewed by query complexity (Dirichlet α = 0.1). Evaluation is on BIRD dev and Spider test, whose databases no client holds. After four rounds, FedLS-SQL reaches 48.76% EX on BIRD dev against 42.57% for FedAvg SFT, and 78.20% on Spider test against 74.71%. Both differences are significant under an exact McNemar test with Holm correction. Further results cover:
- perturbed Spider sets and test-suite accuracy;
- a breakdown of errors (execution errors fall by about half; wrong-result predictions do not fall);
- per-round client cost (0.9–2.7× an SFT round);
- an ablation of pair renewal, the two loss terms, warm-up and aggregation weights.

## 2. Contribution and novelty

Each component is established; what is new is the setting.
- **Iterative on-policy DPO + NLL with execution or correctness labels** is CodeLutra (Tao et al., cited [13]) and Iterative RPO (Pang et al., [22]). The authors say so openly (§2.2, §3.4).
- **Execution-labeled DPO for Text-to-SQL** is in Liu et al. [20] and ExCoT [21].
- **FedAvg + LoRA fine-tuning and FedDPO** are in OpenFedLLM [8] and FederatedScope-LLM [7].
- **Federated training with a verifiable reward** is in Fed-GRPO [25] and PubSwap [24], on math and code.

The contribution is to run CodeLutra-style iterative preference training inside FedAvg rounds, with the global model of the round as the shared reference policy and execution done locally. The authors argue convincingly that execution labels can only be computed where the database lives, so federated Text-to-SQL has a natural on-policy signal that FedAvg SFT ignores. Table 1 positions the work fairly. I know of no prior federated Text-to-SQL work that uses execution feedback, and the "first" claim (l. 86) looks defensible, though the authors should check the 2025–2026 literature again before resubmitting.

The methodological novelty is therefore incremental: a sensible combination, not a new algorithm. Nothing in the method is specific to federation beyond (i) π_ref = θ^{t−1} and (ii) pair-count aggregation weights. Table 8 shows that (ii) makes no measurable difference. For a journal paper, an incremental contribution of this kind is acceptable only if the empirical study is complete and carefully controlled. As submitted, it is not (see §5).

The related work also misses two lines that bear directly on the design:
- **Rejection-sampling self-training** (STaR, RFT, ReST-style SFT on execution-correct own samples). The "Without the preference term" ablation is exactly this, and it is the most important alternative explanation of the gain (Major 3).
- **Online/iterative DPO** in general, beyond Pang et al.

## 3. Soundness of method and experiments

**Method.** The method is clearly specified and internally consistent. Algorithm 1, Eqs. (1)–(4), the pairing rule and the labeling rules (truncated samples not labeled; databases without rows excluded; gold queries that fail are excluded) are precise. The NLL term is properly motivated by the known failure mode of DPO, where both log-likelihoods fall. The reported adapter size (73.9 MB) matches rank-16 LoRA on all seven projection matrices of a 28-layer Qwen2.5-1.5B in fp32, which is a good sign of care.

**Setup.** The client simulation is reasonable and well documented:
- one database per client;
- complexity skew via Dirichlet over hardness tiers;
- evaluation on unseen databases.

Partial participation is absent, and every client participates every round. The authors acknowledge this.

**Statistics.** The exact McNemar test with Holm correction within table families is appropriate for paired predictions, and the authors apply it consistently. They also avoid claiming significance where it fails. That discipline is a strength of the paper. However, McNemar captures only evaluation-sample variance. Almost everything rests on one training seed and one partition draw. The second-seed rows, the only check on training variance, are empty. LoRA + DPO runs at 1.5B scale can vary by more than a point between seeds, so many of the ablation differences of 0.2–2 points cannot be interpreted without them.

**Does the evidence support each main claim?**

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| FedLS-SQL > FedAvg SFT at equal rounds on BIRD dev and Spider test | Supported for one seed and one partition (Table 3, Fig. 2). Large effect, very small p. |
| Comparison with the other baselines (Local SFT, Centralized SFT on BIRD, FedProx SFT, FedDPO) | **Not supported: all entries are missing** (Table 3, l. 387–392). |
| Gain transfers to other models and federated setups (RQ2) | **Mostly unsupported.** Only Qwen-0.5B on Spider is reported. DeepSeek-Coder and all three extra partitions (Table 5) are empty. |
| Robustness on perturbed sets and under TS (RQ3) | Partially supported. The direction holds everywhere, but only Spider-Syn is significant after correction. |
| Error-type analysis and cost (RQ4) | Supported, and analyzed with care. |
| Each component matters (ablation) | Partly. The NLL term is clearly needed on Spider. Pair renewal matters on BIRD. The preference term's EX contribution is **not established**. The warm-up contribution is established only in TS. Several ablation cells are empty, including the 4-round "without preference term," BIRD warm-up variants and "clients pooled." |
| "Second seed" robustness | **Not supported: empty.** |

## 4. Clarity and presentation

The prose is precise and the definitions are careful. Limitations are stated honestly, notably §7.2 and the discussion of spurious-equivalence queries. Several features make the paper harder to read than it should be:
- **Repetitive, very long sentences.** The style restates definitions instead of referring back ("the gold SQL query of a question" appears dozens of times). Sections 5–7 re-report the same numbers several times (e.g., 128 vs 268 execution errors appears in the abstract, intro, §5.4 and §7.1).
- **No plain statement of the main takeaways in the discussion.** §7.1 is a re-listing of tests.
- **Fig. 2 and Fig. 3** are informative, but Fig. 3 would be easier to read as stacked numbers in a table, given how much the text relies on exact counts.
- **The manuscript is visibly unfinished:**
  - the title is "title to be set" and the author block is a template;
  - the FedProx coefficient (l. 336) and many in-text numbers (l. 379–380, 387–392, 416–424, 506, 547) are "??";
  - roughly half the result cells in Tables 3, 4, 5 and 8 are "–";
  - Funding, Author Contributions, Data Availability and Conflicts of Interest read "To be completed."

  The paper promises release of code, splits and predictions (l. 92), but gives no link.

## 5. Major issues

**1. The manuscript is incomplete, and the missing results are the ones needed to evaluate it.**
- RQ1's baselines other than FedAvg SFT are absent: Local SFT, FedProx SFT, FedDPO, and Centralized SFT on BIRD.
- RQ2 is nearly empty.
- The second-seed check and several ablations are missing.

Three of the four listed contributions (l. 88–92) depend on these. Centralized SFT on BIRD and the "clients pooled" FedLS-SQL row are essential: they show how much of the gap to centralized training is closed and what federation costs the method.
*Resolution:* complete every table and in-text placeholder, and give the FedProx coefficient and how it was chosen.

**2. The FedAvg SFT baseline may be under-tuned, which could inflate the gain.**
- FedAvg SFT peaks at round 3 and declines at round 4 on BIRD (43.68 → 42.57) and at round 5 on Spider. That suggests overfitting or an aggressive schedule: LR 2 × 10⁻⁴ with a fresh cosine schedule and optimizer every round.
- The preference rounds use a 4× smaller LR (5 × 10⁻⁵), more LR warm-up and no dropout. So "rounds 3–4" differ in optimization settings as well as in the objective.
- 42.57% on BIRD dev is low for SFT of a 1.5B code model with external knowledge. CodeS-1B, for example, reports around 50% after centralized SFT.

*Resolution:*
- (a) Tune FedAvg SFT, at least LR and local epochs, on a held-out split of the training clients.
- (b) Add a control in which rounds 3–4 are FedAvg SFT run with the preference-round optimization settings.
- (c) Report Centralized SFT on BIRD with the same recipe, to show the SFT recipe is competitive.

**3. The role of the preference term, the paper's central mechanism, is not established in EX.**
- After one preference round (Table 8, 3 rounds), NLL-only training on the model's own correct samples reaches 44.39 vs 46.22 on BIRD and 75.87 vs 77.46 on Spider. Neither difference is significant after correction (adjusted p = 0.46 and 0.14).
- That variant is federated rejection-sampling self-training, a much simpler method, and on BIRD it already accounts for about half the one-round gain over the SFT model (42.70 → 44.39 → 46.22).
- The 4-round version of this variant is missing.

The paper's own evidence fits this reading: the main effect of the preference term is fewer execution errors, offset by more wrong-result predictions (l. 513–518, 554–557). That is a shift between error types, not clearly a gain in correctness.
*Resolution:*
- Report the 4-round NLL-only variant on both datasets with two or more seeds.
- Discuss rejection-sampling self-training (STaR, RFT, ReST) as related work and as a baseline.
- Recalibrate the framing, which currently presents DPO on execution-labeled pairs as the source of the gain, to whatever the completed ablation shows.

**4. Training variance is not assessed.**
All conclusions rest on one training seed and one partition draw (acknowledged at l. 602–604), and McNemar does not account for either. Many ablation conclusions turn on differences under 1 point (e.g., warm-up variants at 78.02 / 78.20 / 77.97; aggregation weights). Some of them may also be suspicious: the question-count and pair-count weightings give identical BIRD EX (46.22 and 46.22, p = 1.00) despite weights differing by up to 2.1 points.
*Resolution:*
- Run the main comparison with at least 3 seeds, and ideally 2–3 partition draws, on both datasets.
- Report mean ± s.d. alongside the McNemar tests.
- Confirm that the identical aggregation-weight result is not a pipeline artifact.

**5. Compute-matched comparison is incomplete.**
A preference round costs 2.7× and 2.3× a FedAvg SFT round on BIRD, and 1.6× and 0.9× on Spider. The equal-compute check (a fifth SFT round) is done only on Spider, where the overhead is smallest.
*Resolution:* on BIRD, give FedAvg SFT the same total client time, about 7 rounds, or show the BIRD curve out to that budget. Given Major 2, this should use a tuned schedule.

**6. Baselines from the closest lines of work are missing.**
- Federated RL with an execution reward (Fed-GRPO-style, cited [25]) is the obvious competitor, given that SLM-SQL, Think2SQL and Arctic-Text2SQL-R1 all use GRPO-type training for small Text-to-SQL models.
- A centralized CodeLutra-style run (the "clients pooled" row) is the natural upper bound.
- FedDPO is only defined; its results are missing.

*Resolution:* add a federated GRPO baseline under the same compute, and complete FedDPO and "clients pooled."

**7. Execution-label noise in training is not quantified.**
Labels come from one database. BIRD compares results as sets, so duplicate rows are ignored. The authors show that more correct test predictions read other tables or columns than the gold query (136 → 191 on BIRD). Mislabelled positives feed back into later rounds.
*Resolution:*
- On a sample of training questions, estimate how many samples labeled correct are not semantically equivalent to the gold query (e.g., by test-suite-style database perturbation, or by manual inspection).
- Report this per round.

The paper's first proposed future direction, labeling on several database instances, could be tested cheaply on Spider, where test suites already exist.

**8. The warm-up design is weakly supported.**
On Spider, EX is the same with 0, 2 or 3 warm-up rounds. The only significant effect is lower TS on Spider-Syn without warm-up. BIRD results, where warm-up should matter most (4,035 questions without a correct sample from the pretrained model), are missing. The alternative of pairing the gold query as the positive was tested only in the no-warm-up variant.
*Resolution:*
- Complete the BIRD warm-up rows.
- Test "gold as positive for questions without a correct sample" within the standard T_w = 2 configuration.

## 6. Minor issues

1. **Title and authors.** Fill in the title and the author block. "Version November 27, 2026submitted" is missing a space in every running header.
2. **Back matter.** Complete Funding, Author Contributions, Data Availability (with a code/data link, as promised at l. 92) and Conflicts of Interest.
3. **BIRD time limit (l. 290–291).** A single 30 s limit for the prediction and the gold query together is non-standard. State whether any gold queries time out under it, and how many predictions are affected compared with the official evaluator.
4. **Query extraction (l. 286–289).** Cutting at the first semicolon and dropping text before the first `SELECT` is generous post-processing. Report how often it changes the outcome, at least for the pretrained model.
5. **Hardness labels on BIRD train.** These come from applying Spider's criteria to sqlglot parses. Give the share of BIRD queries that sqlglot fails to parse, and say how they are assigned.
6. **Adapter transfer.** The adapter is sent in fp32 (73.9 MB). Half precision would halve communication. Mention it.
7. **Table 3.** Δ for "No training" is computed against FedAvg SFT, so the sign convention differs from the other rows. Consider a separate column.
8. **Table 6.** On Spider-DK, FedAvg SFT (59.63) is below no training (60.19), and the no-warm-up variant (67.10) is well above FedLS-SQL (63.55). Comment on this.
9. **"Same number of rounds" (abstract, l. 13–14).** State in the abstract that a preference round costs up to 2.7× a FedAvg SFT round in client time, so readers do not read "rounds" as "compute."
10. **Fig. 1.** It reads "N = 10 SQL per question," but M = 2 pairs is not shown. Adding it would make the cost discussion easier to follow.
11. **Privacy (§7.2).** Pair counts used as aggregation weights reveal per-client model accuracy. Since question-count weighting performs the same, recommend it as the default.
12. **Repeated numbers.** Reduce the repetition of identical numbers across the abstract, intro, §5, §6 and §7. §7.1 could be shortened substantially.
13. **Generative-AI disclosure.** The acknowledgment discloses use of an AI system for drafting text and writing experiment code. Move this to the section the journal's policy specifies, if it has one.

## 7. Recommendation: **Reject** (resubmission encouraged)

Four reasons decide it:

1. **The submission is unfinished.** The title, authors, a hyperparameter, most baselines (Local SFT, FedProx, FedDPO, Centralized SFT on BIRD), nearly all of RQ2, the second seed and several ablations are placeholders. I cannot assess claims whose evidence is missing, and three of the four stated contributions depend on it.
2. **The one complete comparison, against FedAvg SFT, is not yet convincing as an attribution.** The baseline shows signs of under-tuning and uses different optimization settings in the comparison rounds (Major 2). Training variance is unassessed (Major 4).
3. **The paper's own ablation does not establish that the preference term, its central mechanism, improves EX over plain self-training on execution-correct samples** (Major 3).
4. **Methodological novelty is incremental**, a federated version of CodeLutra/Iterative RPO. The empirical study must therefore carry the paper, and it currently cannot.

The idea is sound and well motivated, and the parts that are done show unusual care in definitions and statistics. A completed version that addresses Majors 1–4, ideally with a federated GRPO baseline and some measure of label noise, could make a solid contribution to this journal, and I would be glad to review it.
